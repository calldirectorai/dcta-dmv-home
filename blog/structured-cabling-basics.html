Structured Cabling Basics: Cat5e, Cat6 and Fiber Optic

A plain-language guide to the cables and parts behind every network, written for beginners and career changers.

Home / Blog / Structured cabling basics
Published Nov 17, 2026 · 5 min read · By Data Center Tech Academy DMV

Structured cabling is the organized system of cables, connectors and equipment that carries network and telecom signals through a building. If you are curious about cable work, this is the core idea behind it. This guide explains the basics, including Cat5e, Cat6 and fiber optic cable, in plain language.

The short version: structured cabling is a neat, standard way to connect a building. Copper cables such as Cat5e and Cat6 usually reach desks and devices, fiber optic cable handles longer or busier links, and patch panels and racks keep it all organized and easy to fix.

What structured cabling means

Think of it as the roads of a building. Instead of random wires running everywhere, a structured system follows a planned layout. Cables run from work areas back to a central room, end at labeled ports and connect to equipment in racks. Because everything is organized, problems are easier to trace and changes are easier to make.

This is a big part of what a low voltage technician does. If you are new to the trade, start with what a low voltage technician is.

The main parts of a system

  • Cables. Copper and fiber optic cable carry the signals.
  • Jacks and connectors. These are the ends where a cable plugs in. Installers terminate, or attach, them to each cable.
  • Patch panels. A panel in a rack where cable runs end, so each run has a labeled port.
  • Patch cords. Short cables that connect panel ports to network equipment.
  • Racks and cabinets. The frames that hold panels and equipment in order, often called rack and stack.
  • Labels and documentation. Clear labels so the next person knows where every run goes.

Cat5e cable

Cat5e is a twisted-pair copper cable. The wires inside are twisted in pairs, which helps reduce interference. It has been widely used for networks in offices, schools and homes, and you will still meet it on many sites.

Cat6 cable

Cat6 is also twisted-pair copper, built to tighter specifications than Cat5e. That helps it handle higher speeds and reduces interference in many setups. Installers often choose it for newer projects, but the right choice depends on the plan, the equipment and the budget, so follow the project specifications.

Fiber optic cable

Fiber optic cable carries light through very thin strands of glass instead of electricity through copper. It is commonly used for longer runs, links between rooms or buildings, and busy data connections. Fiber needs careful handling and its own tools and testing, so it is worth learning step by step.

Copper vs fiber at a glance

  • Copper (Cat5e, Cat6). Common for the final run to a desk, phone or device.
  • Fiber. Common for longer or higher-demand links between equipment areas.

Many buildings use both together. A technician who understands each can work on more kinds of jobs.

Why testing matters

A cable that looks fine can still fail. Technicians use testers to check that each run is wired correctly and works as expected, then fix any problems before the job is done. Testing and troubleshooting are skills employers look for, and they are easy to practice in a lab. For a wider view of the field, see how to become a low voltage technician with no experience.

Common questions

Q01

What is structured cabling?

Structured cabling is a planned, organized system of cables, connectors, patch panels and racks that carries network and telecom signals through a building. It is built to a standard layout so it is easy to test, label, fix and expand.
Q02

What is the difference between Cat5e and Cat6?

Both are twisted-pair copper cables used for networks. Cat6 is built to tighter specifications than Cat5e, which helps it handle higher speeds and reduces interference over certain distances. Which one a job needs depends on the plan and the equipment, so installers follow the project specifications.
Q03

When is fiber optic cable used instead of copper?

Fiber optic cable sends light instead of electrical signals. It is commonly used for long runs, links between buildings or rooms, and busy data connections, while copper is common for the final run to a desk or device.
Q04

Do I need experience to learn structured cabling?

No. Many people start with no experience. Hands-on practice with termination, patch panels and testing is the best way to build the skills, and training programs for beginners exist for that reason.
