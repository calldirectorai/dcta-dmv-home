What Is a Low Voltage Technician? What They Do and How to Start

What the job involves, where they work, and how beginners get started.

Home / Blog / Low voltage technician
Published October 13, 2026 · 5 min read · By Data Center Tech Academy DMV

A low voltage technician installs, tests and repairs the cabling and equipment that carry data, voice, video and security signals inside buildings. If you have ever plugged into a wall jack, watched a security camera feed or used a badge reader, a technician like this set it up.

The short version: low voltage work is hands-on, in demand across many kinds of buildings, and open to beginners. You do not need a degree to start, but you do need to learn the basics of cabling, termination, testing and safety.

What does "low voltage" mean?

Low voltage refers to systems that run on much less power than the electrical wiring that feeds a building's outlets and lights. Instead of carrying power to appliances, these systems carry information: internet and network traffic, telephone and telecom connections, video, alarms and access control.

What does a low voltage technician do every day?

The work varies by employer, but a typical week can include:

  • Pulling cable through ceilings, walls and conduit from an equipment room to each work area.
  • Terminating cable on RJ45 connectors, keystone jacks and patch panels so each connection works reliably.
  • Testing and labeling every run so the next person can find and trust it.
  • Building and organizing racks in network closets and data centers, often called rack and stack.
  • Installing devices such as wireless access points, cameras, door readers and phones.
  • Troubleshooting when a connection drops, a port is dead or a run was never labeled.
  • Reading floor plans and drawings to know where everything goes.

Where do low voltage technicians work?

Almost every building with technology needs this work: offices, schools, hospitals, warehouses, apartment buildings, retail stores and data centers. Many technicians work for structured cabling contractors, security and systems integrators, telecom companies or an in-house facilities team. In the Maryland, DC and Northern Virginia area there is a steady mix of government, healthcare, commercial and technology sites.

Skills and tools you will use

  • Structured cabling basics: Cat5e, Cat6 and fiber optic cable
  • Termination tools such as punch-down tools, crimpers and strippers
  • Cable testers and basic troubleshooting methods
  • Safe work habits: ladders, tight spaces, tools and job-site awareness
  • Neat, consistent work and good labeling
  • Clear communication with customers and coworkers

Low voltage technician or electrician?

They are different trades that often share a job site. Electricians work on a building's main power wiring. Low voltage technicians work on the data, communications and security systems that run alongside it. Some rules and licensing requirements can vary depending on the type of work and where you do it, so check the requirements for the kind of work you want to do.

Can a beginner become a low voltage technician?

Yes. Many people enter the field without prior experience, including career changers, recent graduates and people looking for a trade with room to grow. What matters early on is that you can show you understand the fundamentals and work safely. Pay depends on the employer, your experience and your location, so be skeptical of anyone promising a specific figure.

Common questions

Q01

Is a low voltage technician the same as an electrician?

No. Electricians generally work on the building's main power systems. Low voltage technicians work on the systems that carry data, signal and communications, like network cabling, phones, cameras and access control. The two trades often work on the same job site, and the rules for each can differ, so check your local requirements.
Q02

Do I need a college degree to become a low voltage technician?

No degree is required to get started in most entry-level cabling work. Employers tend to look for reliability, comfort with hands-on work, basic safety habits and the ability to learn. Training that shows you can terminate and test cable can help you stand out.
Q03

Is low voltage work physically demanding?

It can be. Technicians pull cable, climb ladders, work in tight spaces such as ceilings and equipment rooms, and are on their feet much of the day. Many people find the variety and the hands-on problem solving a good change from desk work.
Q04

How long does it take to learn the basics?

The core skills of cable pulling, termination and testing can be learned in a short, focused program. At DCTA DMV the Cable Technician Training Program is four weeks, with morning and evening classes. Becoming fast and confident takes practice on real jobs after that.
Q05
